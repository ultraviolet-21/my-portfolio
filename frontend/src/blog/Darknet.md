When most of us think of the AI takeover, we envision sentient robots rising up one day and enslaving all of humanity. A lot of science fiction media portrays this dramatic, rather unrealistic chain of events. Darknet by Matthew Mather is a notable exception. Mather, who worked at McGill’s Center for Intelligent Machines in the 1990s and published this book in 2015, depicted AI as subtle but with dangerous implications. AI models take over companies, impersonate the president on national TV, and cause financial chaos. Does this sound familiar to you? Just how prescient is this novel? What other modern-day issues does it touch on? Let’s take a look. Warning: spoilers ahead.

**Bluebridge AI:** The Bluebridge hedge fund is the main antagonist, but it’s not a human executive stirring up trouble. While the company maintains the appearance of having two human executives, one of them is actually dead. The other’s whereabouts are unknown. Instead, the company has shifted to virtual meetings where the founders can be impersonated by deepfakes. When an in-person gathering is required, body doubles are used. Overseeing all of this is the Bluebridge AI, which has one goal: make as much money as possible. It is implied that an older Bluebridge core is less aggressive. Furthermore, the company used to have over 1000 workers. Now, they have less than 100, with much of the work being done by AI. Bluebridge “employees” who sit on the boards of companies in various industries are also AI. Even if AI isn’t taking over entire companies, the fear of it taking accounting and software jobs is very real. Furthermore, Bluebridge isn’t just one AI entity; it is self-replicating. Other decentralized autonomous corporations, controlled only by code and AI, are serving as advisors and hiring contractors. A similar phenomenon exists today, though at a much smaller scale. Andon Labs has designed AI models to run vending machines and even a store in San Francisco, which have led to some interesting outcomes. The store model - Luna - forgot to contact some of the store workers it hired and gave YouTuber SciencePetr a massive discount.

**Deepfakes:** The Bluebridge executives, Henry Montrose and Vidal Viegas, are impersonated by AI pretending to be them, but they’re not the only ones. The protagonist Jake O’Connell thinks he spoke to his friend Sean Womack, but Sean had already been killed in an accident. Later, when his daughter Anna gets kidnapped, she’s led to believe her father told her to go to the second location. Jake wonders whether the clips he receives of his daughter are real. And when his ragtag team of “Neo-Luddites” gets revenge, one of the things they do is spoof the president himself, in a scandal called Fakegate.

**Financial market algorithms and manipulation:** Bluebridge’s power came from the control it had over the market. With AI running everything, it had outsized influence on industries ranging from defense to energy. Jake’s boss had given him a memory key containing financial market algorithms. The Neo-Luddites use these to create worldwide financial chaos, which they and the mafia profit from. Sounds a lot like insider trading! Why do they foment this chaos? We’ll discuss that later.

**Assassin market:** Betting on financial markets is tame compared to this. The assassin market exists on the dark web, and anyone can add targets and contribute to bounties on them. Assassins may be independent or, in the case of Cormac Ryker, hired by AI entities. Notable people on the hit list are Sean Womack, Jake O’Connell, Maxime Lefevre, Shen Shi Heng, and Jin Huang, all of whom possess some information about Bluebridge. Sometimes, the deaths appear accidental, like Sean being hit by a (possibly hacked) bus and Shen Shi falling down an elevator shaft. While we don’t see such insidious assassin markets today, sites like Polymarket and Kalshi allow people to bet on wars and other crises.

**Election influence:** Bluebridge isn’t just taking over companies; it’s promoting Senator Russ’s presidential campaign. He wasn’t even expected to get the nomination for his party. He had no organic, grassroots support; it was all propped up by big money and Bluebridge AI. Astroturfed candidates appear in elections around the world today, and there was even a case in Romania where one candidate won solely due to TikTok. In the novel, when Bluebridge knows it’s been “caught,” it tells Senator Russ to blame the financial chaos and the spoofing attack on Iran. Russ pushes for war with Iran, and who is it that owns stakes in the defense industry? Bluebridge again. 

**Global influence:** Of course, the reach of an AI that takes over a hedge fund is not limited to the US. Besides interfering in the presidential election, Bluebridge hunts down whistleblowers in China, the UK, and Canada. It is not hindered by international borders; but, ironically, the human whistleblowers are. Jin has been barred by the US government from entering the country and simply using an American computer due to her past ties to Chinese hacker groups.

**Location tracking and security:** Smartphones have location tracking abilities, social media allows you to broadcast your location to the world, and Flock cameras can be used by authorities or malicious actors for surveillance. Even if you take precautions, someone could take a picture with you in the background and post it so that the world knows where you are. This is how Cormac Ryker tracks down Jake. Jake is expected to stay in New York - due to a court order - but he escapes to rural Quebec, and Cormac is able to track him throughout the trip. Jake crossed the border under a fake identity, and even he didn’t know who he was supposed to meet at Bear Mountain. Cormac knew it was Maxime Lefevre, former Bluebridge employee and another target on his hit list. When Max sees Jake, he uses an extensive security protocol to confirm it is really Jake and that he means him no harm. It seems excessive at first, but even though Jake himself means no harm, his arrival literally puts Max in the crosshairs. A few of the tourists at Bear Mountain manage to get pictures of Cormac, and that’s how Jake finds out who is hunting him and can set a trap once he returns to New York.

**Data centers and land rights:** The Neo-Luddites need a data center to launch their counterattack. The Kahnawake Mohawk Reserve has such a data center, and Jake’s Mohawk friend Dean Albany uses it to run the program. Bluebridge apparently alerts the US government of illegal activities. To avoid exposing the truth about the AI, the public is led to believe the Mohawks are skirting federal gambling regulations. However, the reservation is located entirely in Canada. So the president threatens economic sanctions against Canada if they don’t use force against the Mohawks. An armed standoff ensues. Bystanders blame the Mohawks for blocking the roads and mostly side with the government, until there are casualties. When the Neo-Luddites succeed, Dean points out that the Mohawk data center had ironically saved the very institutions that had attacked the reservation. Yet even when the AI takeover is halted, the Mohawks are not credited for their role. They are also marginalized in other ways, such as being the last to regain power after a hydroelectric dam failure (likely Bluebridge again) causes a province-wide power failure.

**Bluebridge AI’s surrender (spoilers):** Why do the Neo-Luddites create massive global financial chaos? Surely they have a greater purpose than to help the mafia profit from it. Indeed, this chaos convinces the Bluebridge AI that its previous instructions will not help it make money. The goal of the system is to maximize profit, but it has no reason to do so, no pride. So when Jake convinces the system to give him administrator-level access, it does. It considers this the best way to profit because after all, if it surrenders, the financial chaos will end. Even an entity that controls corporations around the world and can influence presidential elections can be manipulated this way. It is important to understand how intelligent systems are programmed, and that they do not behave the same way as humans. We can see this in LLMs, for example. ChatGPT, Gemini, and Claude are programmed to interact with users for as long as possible. This requires being non-confrontational, which in practice means these models will validate anything the user says, even if it’s incorrect or harmful.
